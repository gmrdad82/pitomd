Every system I ever abandoned, I abandoned at the same doorstep: the
import screen. Somewhere in my late twenties I accepted a truth about
myself — I will not maintain a second home for my thoughts. The first
home already existed. It was a folder called `notes`, full of markdown
files, growing since before some of the apps that wanted to replace it
were founded. It had survived four laptops, two operating systems, one
divorce from a cloud provider, and every single one of my moods. The
folder was never the problem. The folder was the only thing that
worked.

What the folder could not do was answer questions. It held everything
and told me nothing. I would stand in front of it like a man in front
of a garage he has filled for a decade: the thing I need is in there,
I put it there myself, and the only retrieval tool I own is patience.

## The markdown notes app I refused to want

I did not want an app. I want to be clear about that, because this is
an origin story and origin stories get romantic. I wanted the folder,
exactly as it was, to become answerable.

The push came from a small command-line tool called qmd. I will name
it openly because credit is owed: qmd searched plain markdown files by
meaning, right there on the machine, no service attached. You typed
what you remembered — not the words you wrote, the thing you meant —
and it found the file. The first time it pulled up the right note from
a half-remembered thought, I laughed out loud, alone, at a terminal.
It was such a small tool, and it moved something enormous: it proved
that intelligence could come to the files, instead of the files being
shipped off to the intelligence.

That proof rearranged my head. Every notes product I had ever tried
got the direction wrong. They all said: bring your thoughts to us, and
we will make them smart. qmd said: your thoughts are fine where they
are; smartness travels. Once I had seen the direction reversed, I
could not unsee it — and I could not stop wanting more of it. If
search could come to the folder, so could tasks. So could structure.
So could a whole desk.

I looked for that app for a while. What I found were importers with
better marketing. So the folder and I made a decision.

## What an idea deserves

Said and Done. is the UI I believe that idea deserved. Not a wrapper
around a script — a real desk, built with the patience you reserve for
a thing you will use every day for the rest of your life.

The idea stayed pure on the way in, and the purity is measurable. My
folder is still a folder. The files are still files, editable in
anything, owned by me in the oldest sense — they are just bytes on my
disk. Every top-level folder became a Notebook without being asked
twice; inside them I got Chapters and Pages, tasks that point into the
files at the exact lines that matter, and search that understands what
I meant — the qmd trick, grown up, running on my own graphics card
with a small model that ships inside the installer. That last part is
an honest requirement, so I will say it plainly: the desk needs a real
GPU. The meaning search runs on it, and a machine without one is told
so at the door instead of being strung along.

Nothing about that paragraph required my notes to move, convert,
enroll, or trust anyone. That was the whole test. Any feature that
could not be built on top of the untouched folder did not get built.

## Ten years of notes, day one of the desk

The reward for starting where the notes already are arrived on the
first day, and it is the part no import wizard can fake: history.

A new app starts you at zero. Empty screens, sample text, a sad little
"create your first note" button. Said and Done. started me at ten
years. The moment it pointed at my folder, the meaning search indexed
a decade of me — old plans, old prices, old measurements, the
paragraph where I talked myself out of a motorcycle. My second-ever
search in the desk found a note from six years earlier and pinned it
to a task I wrote that morning. Six years of dormancy, and the thought
was suddenly load-bearing again.

That is what your existing notes are: not baggage to migrate, but
capital to activate. Every app that asks you to start fresh is quietly
asking you to write off that capital. The folder-first direction means
the oldest, dustiest note you own is exactly as reachable as the one
you wrote today — and when a new task turns out to lean on it, the
desk makes the connection whether or not you remembered the note
exists.

There is a quieter reward, too, and it compounds: no exit anxiety.
Because the desk never absorbed my notes, leaving it would cost me
nothing — the folder simply remains, the way it remained through every
app before. I know how strange it sounds for a builder to advertise
how little his app holds hostage. That is the point. An app you can
leave freely is an app you can trust with your daily thinking, and
trust, not features, is what a notes system runs on. I stopped
auditioning apps the day I stopped being asked to move in. My notes
never went anywhere; everything else finally came to them.

If your own folder is sitting there right now — old, plain, faithful,
unanswerable — that is not a system waiting to be replaced. That is a
desk waiting to be built on. Mine took years to arrive. Yours is a
download away, and it will find you exactly where your notes already
are.
