Every app opens empty, and empty is a liar. The screenshots showed a
bustling workspace; your screen shows a ghost town and a button politely
begging for your first note. You cannot tell whether search is any good
with three notes in it. You cannot judge a system you have to build
before you may try it. So the trial becomes homework, and the
homework becomes the reason the app gets deleted on day two.

I refused to let Said and Done. open empty. The first time you launch
it, there is already a world inside: an expedition called Mars or Bust.
Two people, one ship, two hundred files. It is my favorite thing in the
whole app, and it exists so you can move to Mars first and only unpack
your own life once you like the neighborhood.

One thing to check before you pack: Said and Done. needs a real
graphics card, because the meaning search runs a local model on it. A
machine without one will say so plainly at the door rather than let you
down later.

## A sample world with an actual plot

Mars or Bust is not filler text in a costume. I wrote it, slowly, the
way you write anything you want a stranger to enjoy finding. It is a
hand-written expedition: two colonists working their way from a salvaged ship to a
livable homestead, and every file reads like they meant it. There are
flight logs and fuel calculations. A cargo manifest with opinions. Field
notes from the surface, materials research, salvage inventories, workshop
plans — real markdown files, sitting in a real folder on your disk, in a
sample Notebook you can open and read like a short story told through
paperwork.

On top of that folder stands the desk in full working order: dozens of
Pages across the expedition's Chapters, each one a task the colonists
plausibly face. And the Pages come pinned, wired to the exact
paragraphs of the notes they depend on, precisely the way your own tasks
will be once your files move in. The refit Page points into the ship
notes. The greenhouse Pages lean on the materials research. Somebody has
to fix the water situation, and the Page about it knows exactly which
lines of which file explain how bad things are.

The margins are already alive, too. Some Pages carry Updates from AI
agents — a quartermaster, a surveyor — discussing cargo and terrain under
their own names, so you can see what signed, multi-hand collaboration
looks like on a desk before you invite your own helpers in. A few Pages
even carry the expedition's drawings: hand-drawn schematics of the dew
condenser, the tunnel garden, the route from Earth. Attachments,
demonstrated by attached things worth opening.

## Try every feature on somebody else's life

The sample world has a second job: it makes every feature
demonstrable in your first ten minutes, with stakes belonging entirely to
fictional people.

Search for something you never read — try "keeping plants alive in bad
soil" — and watch results come back by meaning, from files you have not
opened, because the expedition's notes are indexed the moment the world
arrives. Open a Page and press the key that shows its pinned lines;
widen the view; feel what it is to read a file _from_ a task. Pull up
the Chapter overview and watch the map draw itself — Pages and files in
one picture, showing which notes the colony's work leans on.
Walk a Page through its steps. Delete one and hear the glass break,
which I will not explain further, because some things you should meet
in person.

The expedition is also where your hands learn the desk. Said and Done.
is built to be driven from the keyboard, and a keyboard-driven anything
has a first awkward week, so spend it on Mars. A quiet ticker under the
record tells you what the keys do right where you are, and the help sheet
is generated from the actual live keymap, which means it cannot drift
into fiction the way printed manuals do. Practice hopping Page to Page,
jumping by letter, stepping Pages forward and back, all on the colony's
chores. By the time your own Notebooks arrive, your fingers know the way
around, and they learned it on tasks that could not be hurt.

Fumbling is free here in every other sense too. Move Pages somewhere
stupid. Delete something and undo it. Reword a note and watch the pins
re-grip. You are a guest in a world built to be rummaged through, and
nothing you break belongs to anyone real. When the tour is over — or
when your own Notebooks have long since taken over and you want the
guest room back — Settings will reseed the whole expedition to mint
condition with one action.

Two promises make the guest room permanent. The sample never counts
against the free plan's limits: its Notebooks, Chapters and Pages are on
the house, forever. And it never stops working, licensed or not, so there is
always one complete, fully furnished corner of the app where everything
can be tried, tested, and shown to the skeptical friend looking over
your shoulder.

## Mars was never the subject

Somewhere in the middle of reading two colonists argue about water
reclamation, the thing I actually built the sample for happens: you stop
evaluating and start recognizing. Their scattered files and stubborn
little tasks look suspiciously like your `house/` folder, your someday
projects, your latch that sticks.

Read the manifest.
Break the glass. Take the tour twice if you like; the ship reseeds. Your
own Pages can take their time arriving, because the neighborhood has
already shown you what living here is like.
