I record more than I should. Game sessions, screen captures, the
occasional family chaos that deserved a camera. The folder where the
footage lands has my old notes folder's exact personality: full, and
silent. Somewhere in forty minutes of Tuesday
is the one minute worth keeping — the goal, the laugh, the sentence
where I finally explained the thing properly — and the traditional way
to find it is to sit there, scrubbing, watching my own life at double
speed like a bored security guard.

I fixed my notes with a desk. It was only a matter of time before the
same beliefs came for my videos. So consider this a tease, openly
labeled: the desk has a sibling, it is called Pito Studio, and it cuts
video the way Said and Done. takes notes.

## What an easy video editor learns from a notes app

Easy, in editors, usually means fewer knobs. That was never what made
the desk easy. The desk is easy because of where it _starts_: your
files, in place, untouched, with intelligence coming to them instead
of the other way around. Studio starts in the exact same spot.

Your recordings stay where they live. Nothing is imported, copied, or
swallowed into a project bundle — the footage is referenced on your
own disk, and if you reorganize your drives someday, relinking keeps
every transcript and clip you made. If you have ever lost an
afternoon to a video editor's idea of "media management", you know
this is not a small courtesy. It is the folder-first principle,
applied to files a thousand times heavier.

Then comes the part that changed how I record: Studio transcribes
everything, on your own graphics card, and the words become the way
in. Search what was said — by the exact phrase, or by meaning, the
same trick the desk plays with notes. "Find my raging moments" is a
real query that works on my machine. The forty minutes of Tuesday
stop being a timeline to scrub and become a document to search, and
finding the minute that matters takes about as long as remembering
roughly what I said during it. Notes taught me that retrieval is the
whole game. Video was never different; video just had worse search.

## The same house rules

The desk's beliefs carry over one by one, and I mean carried over
literally — the same builder, the same stubbornness, pointed at a
harder problem.

Local first, offline, yours. There is no server doing your work and
no account holding your key to it. After the first boot fetches its
tools, the internet is optional; your footage never leaves the
machine, which matters double for video, because footage is the most
personal data most families produce.

Honest about the machine. Like the desk, Studio does real work on a
real GPU and says so plainly, and its built-in doctor checks your
machine before installing anything, installs what is missing with
honest progress, and speaks in plain language when something is
genuinely wrong.

Quit-safe by design. Close Studio mid-transcription, mid-render,
mid-anything — reopen tomorrow and the line re-forms exactly where it
stopped. Nothing restarts from zero, nothing is lost. The desk made
me allergic to apps that punish you for closing them; Studio was
built with the same allergy.

Paid the same honest way. One payment, two machines, every release
of its era, no subscription — and it works offline forever, because
an app you bought should never brick itself over a server's bad day.
The monthly auto-cut tools want a rent check for your own footage.
Studio's whole pricing argument fits in two words: pay once.

Even the AI arrives with the desk's manners. Bring your own key and
Claude will search your spoken words, propose a cut sized to the
video you asked for, and queue the render when told — a named helper
doing legwork on your machine's terms. Skip the key and everything
still works by hand. Assistance, never dependence; the same
arrangement the desk struck with its agents.

## Why I am telling you before it is on the shelf

Partly because the overlap is the point. If the desk's beliefs rang
true for your notes — files stay yours, work happens at home, search
by meaning, pay once, leave freely — then you already know whether
Studio is for you, months before you see a screenshot. Products from
one household should be judgeable this way: meet one sibling, know
the family.

And partly because writing it down keeps me honest. This article is
a promise made in public about what Studio is: fast, joyful about its
own work — it narrates its jobs like a small theater troupe, and its
animations are drawn by math, not stock assets — and respectful of
your files, your machine, and your wallet, in that order. When it
lands on this site properly, with its tour and its download button,
you will be able to hold this piece up against the shipped thing and
check the receipts. That is how the desk was built, and the method is
not changing for the sibling.

Until then, the practical takeaway fits in a sentence: stop letting
your footage rot in an unanswerable folder. Keep recording, keep the
files, and know that the same move that saved my notes — a desk that
comes to the files and makes them searchable by what they _mean_ —
is coming for the heaviest folder you own. My Tuesday recordings are
already a search box instead of a chore.

Yours are next, and the scrubbing days are numbered.
