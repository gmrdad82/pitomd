The gate to my back garden has a latch that sticks, and I have written
about it exactly once, months ago, somewhere in the middle of
`garden/spring-plans.md`, between a paragraph about raised beds and a
diagram I never finished. I know this the way you know things you will
never find again. So when the latch finally lost its last screw and I
stomped inside to write the task, I typed four words into Said and Done. —
"fix the fence gate" — and hit enter, because the dog was still outside
enjoying the newly unguarded exit.

By the time I got back to the desk, the page had a reference pinned to it:
`garden/spring-plans.md`, the exact section where past-me had measured the
latch, named the screws, and noted which hardware store had them. I never
searched for that note. I never attached it. I typed a task, and the desk
did the filing.

## Automatic note linking, at the moment it helps

What happens in that gap between typing and coffee: When you
create a page and attach nothing to it, Said and Done. takes your words
and searches that notebook's own folder: the real markdown files
sitting on your disk. It is not looking for a filename that matches; it
reads for the best hit inside the files, then works out the section
around that hit, the way you would with your thumb holding the page open.
What lands on your page is a proper pin: the right file and the right
lines of it, ready to open with a keypress.

The part I sweated over is restraint. A desk that pins the wrong note is
worse than a desk that pins nothing, because a wrong link wears the
costume of a right one. So the finder holds itself to standards: your
task needs enough words to mean something, the matched section has to be
reasonably sized rather than half a file, and the winning candidate has
to clear a real score before it earns the pin. When nothing clears the
bar, the page simply stays bare, and that is the correct answer. You can
pin something yourself in two keystrokes, or let the page live
unencumbered. In my desk, most tasks that should find their note do,
and the ones that find nothing genuinely had nothing to find, usually
because the note in question still lives only in my head.

What this removes is the filing tax on capture. The old
arithmetic was cruel: writing a task cost five seconds, but writing it
properly — hunting the related note, linking it, filing the thing where
future-me could act on it — cost three minutes, so nine tasks out of ten
got captured badly or not at all. Now the five-second version is the
proper version. The dog's vet page found the note with his medication
history. The tax-season page found the paragraph where I explained last
year's mess to myself. Each time, the connection cost me nothing, and
each time it was the connection — never the four words — that made the
task doable weeks later.

One honest boundary, so you are never surprised: this magic runs at
creation, once. An existing page is never rescanned behind your back;
your desk does not rearrange itself while you sleep. If you write the
note after the task, pin it by hand; the desk assists, it does not
haunt.

## The pin you never made pays you back

The connection the desk made for you at creation keeps working in the
other direction, and this is my favorite trick in the whole app. Because pages pin real files, your notes and your
tasks are no longer two separate piles, so when you search, a hit in one
pile can drag the other pile into the light.

Say I search for "keeping the dog out of the vegetables." The meaning
search — a small model running entirely on my machine — finds the note
where I wrote about fencing off the beds, even though I phrased none of
it that way. Then Said and Done. does the extra step that makes the
feature: it checks which pages have that note pinned, and surfaces those
too. My "order chicken wire" task appears in the results even though the
task itself contains none of the words I searched and none of their
meaning either. It shows up because it is _pinned to the thought_, and
the thought matched.

## A filing clerk you never have to thank

There is a background worker doing all of this, and the app will happily
show you its little jobs board: what is queued, what just ran, whether
the finder is currently reading your garden notes. I check it about as
often as I check my water meter. That is
the point. Filing is precisely the kind of work that should happen where
you cannot see it, done by something that never gets bored, never
misfiles out of spite, and never decides your system needs reinventing.

The latch on my gate
is fixed, by the way. The task knew which screws, because the note knew,
because the pin knew — and the only thing I ever typed was the problem.
