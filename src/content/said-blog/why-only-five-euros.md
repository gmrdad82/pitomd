The honest answer: because the free desk is not a demo. It is the product.

## What free actually holds

Five projects. Ten boards. A hundred cards. Those are the only caps, and
there is no clock on any of it — no trial that expires, no feature that
grays out on day fifteen, no watermark.

Everything else is simply there, for everyone:

- Four lanes and a dossier, on every board.
- The whole keyboard grammar — one mode, no insert/normal ceremony,
  letters that stay quiet while a field has focus.
- Your notes under every card: a card can point at any number of files,
  down to the exact lines, and read them in place.
- Search that spans titles, bodies, and the referenced lines themselves —
  plus on-device meaning search when your machine carries a GPU.
- The remote client: your desk from a phone, served by the same process,
  over a path you own.
- Agents at the desk over MCP, every action signed with the agent's name.
- Backups daily, done properly — an online snapshot, never a raw file
  copy, the last fourteen days kept.
- Every pixel of the same app paying customers run. There is no second,
  nicer binary.

A hundred cards sounds small until you work the way the desk wants you
to: cards resolve. They land in done as done, abandoned, or not
relevant, and the desk files them away while keeping every one findable.
A living board rarely holds a hundred open thoughts — and if yours does,
you are exactly who the paid tier is for.

![Everything the free desk holds, against the one price](/said-and-done/blog/thumbs/why-only-five-euros.png)

## Where the care goes

The reason the free tier feels like more than enough is not generosity,
it is engineering taste. Said and Done. is one Rust process drawing on
your GPU — no browser pretending to be an app, no server pretending to
be your disk. The data is a single SQLite file in your home directory.
Nothing phones home; there is no account to make and no telemetry to
opt out of, because there is nothing collecting.

That is where the €4.99 goes, too: into the unglamorous parts. Pixel
measurements instead of eyeballing. A keyboard law — keys reach only
the topmost screen, ever — enforced in one place so a shortcut can
never ambush you. Backups that use the database's own online backup
instead of hoping a file copy lands between writes. The kind of work
nobody screenshots.

## So why charge at all?

Because the desk should outlive its launch. One payment of €4.99 lifts
every cap, on two devices, for every 1.x release — no subscription,
nothing to renew. You had unlimited time to decide first, which is also
why a purchase is final.

Call it what it is: a token of support from the people who ended up
living in the thing. If the free desk is all you ever need — good.
That was the point. It will keep working, and it will keep getting
better, because a few people who didn't have to pay decided the desk
had said, and done, enough to deserve it.
