My best ideas do not visit me at the desk. They wait — politely,
patiently, like they have read my calendar — for the exact moment my
hands are full of leashes and one of my dogs has taken a controversial
position on a squirrel. Something about walking does it: the legs take
over the thinking-adjacent work, the eyes get sky instead of screens,
and the head, suddenly unsupervised, starts producing. Solutions to
things I stopped working on. The right name for the thing I could not
name Tuesday. Whole paragraphs, delivered free, to a man holding a bag
of something warm.

For years I lost most of them, and I told myself the losing was
natural. Ideas are fish; some get away. Then I did the honest
arithmetic on what a lost idea actually costs — the fence design I
reinvented three times, the game mechanic that came and went twice
before I caught it — and I stopped romanticizing the fish.

## Capturing ideas on the go is a latency problem

An idea in the wild has a shelf life of about ninety seconds. That is
the whole problem, and every solution that ignores it fails. It does
not matter how beautiful your system is back home if the thought dies
in the field — and thoughts die of very small delays. An app that
wants a login. A notes screen that opens to yesterday's note instead
of an empty line. The four seconds of your phone deciding whether it
knows your face. Ninety seconds sounds generous until you subtract
the squirrel negotiations.

So the field rule is: capture must cost less than forgetting. Mine
has two moves, and the first involves no technology at all. The
moment an idea lands, I give it a _handle_ — three or four words,
said once, out loud if the street is empty enough. "Fence gate
counterweight." "Tutorial before the menu." The handle is not the
idea; it is the hook the idea hangs from, and a spoken hook survives
the rest of the walk far better than a whole unspoken paragraph. Any
neuroscientist would wince at my terminology, and any dog-walker can
confirm it works.

The second move happens at the first safe pause: the handle goes into
the phone, raw, no ceremony. Said and Done.'s pocket client opens
ready to type; four words and one tap later the handle is a Page,
named and permanent, before the leash gets pulled again. Total cost: maybe
eight seconds, most of them typos. I am not writing the idea on the
sidewalk. I am buying it a seat home.

## The folder that follows you

The reason four rushed words are enough — instead of a paragraph I
would need a bench to write — is what happens after they land.

The Pages I fling from the field arrive in the same desk as
everything else I think. Not an inbox app that syncs to a someday
app. The actual desk, sitting on the actual folder of notes I have
kept for years. So when "fence gate counterweight" lands, it lands
_near_ every other fence thought I have ever had — and the desk,
which searches my notes for the thinking a new task belongs to,
routinely pins the relevant old paragraph to my four words before I
am home to do it myself. Field-me supplies the spark. The archive
supplies the context. Neither of us could do the other's job.

This is the part I would sell hardest to anyone building their own
version of this habit, with any tools at all: keep one thinking
place, and make your capture flow _into_ it, not beside it. A
separate quick-notes app is where ideas go to be safe and then
irrelevant — safe from forgetting, cut off from everything that
would make them matter. The walk produces sparks precisely because
the head is away from the archive; the system's job is to reunite
them by evening. Mine does it while I am still washing dog off my
hands.

And the evening is where the method quietly pays out. On the couch,
during the day's last slow scroll through the desk, the field
handles get their bodies: a line of description while the context
still breathes, a step forward for the ones with momentum, a
merciful deletion for the handles that turned out to be squirrel
adrenaline. Five minutes. The idea that visited me mid-walk is now a
named, filed, context-wearing Page — indistinguishable from a
thought I had comfortably at the desk, except it is usually better,
because walking ideas usually are.

## Anyone can run this

The two-move method needs no particular app, and I want to be plain
about that: a voice memo and a shoebox would beat what most people
do now, which is trusting the ninety seconds. If you take nothing
else, take the handle trick — it is free and it works in the
shower, in traffic, and in the school pickup line, three other
places ideas famously choose over desks.

What the desk adds is the second half nobody has the discipline to
do by hand: the reuniting. Sparks filed into the same place the
thinking lives, connections made while you walk, nothing owed in
the morning. My part shrank to four words and eight seconds, which
is a price the field always lets me pay, no matter what the dogs
are doing.

The squirrel position, for the record, remains controversial. But
the ideas from that walk are all still alive, every one of them
filed, and one of them is the article you just finished reading —
caught, like the rest, one handle at a time.
