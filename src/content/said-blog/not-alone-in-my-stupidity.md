There is a saying I use on myself: I know I'm alone in my genius, but I
hope I'm not alone in my stupidity. The genius half is the joke — every
household lets you keep one flattering lie. The stupidity half is a
sincere hope, and this article is me testing it in public.

Here is the stupidity. For years — many, many years — I kept my life in
notes apps. Cloud ones. Syncing ones. Apps that synced to other apps
that backed up to a third thing, a quiet bucket brigade passing my own
thoughts around the internet all night. And every one of them failed at
the only job I actually hired it for: getting a thought back, fast,
when I needed it. Search meant exact match — a needle in a haystack,
except you must also remember which word the needle was. If I wrote
"marten" the day the fence lost a board and searched "weasel" a year
later, the note may as well have been buried in the garden it was
about.

## The fence never closes

The fence of my garden is not a task. It is a topic, and it has been
open for years and will stay open for as long as the garden has me. It
goes quiet for whole seasons. Then a storm files a report. Then some
animal — a weasel, a marten, I never caught it in the act — files
another. Each of those arrived in my head while walking the dogs, got
thumbed into whatever app my phone held that year, and became an
orphan: a new note, no family, no history, no idea that eleven older
notes were already saying the same thing in slightly different words.

The apps were not wrong to sync. They were wrong about what the job
was. The job was never to store the fence thoughts. The job was to
know they belong together.

## What the pile could never tell me

No app I ever signed up for could answer the one question that
mattered: how many times, in the last five years, have I told myself
the garage door has a problem? Fifteen? Fifty? Five hundred? Every
note was born alone and stayed alone, so the honest answer was
"scroll and count". A pile that knows everything and can tell you
nothing is not a memory — it is a landfill with timestamps.

## A Page is an address

This is the whole reason the fence lives differently in Said and Done.
The fence is a Page. It was born with a permanent name — Notebook plus
number, GARDEN-12 — and it keeps that name through every quiet season
and every storm. The storm's damage lands on it as a Thought. The
animal report is a Thought. The measurement from the hardware store run
is a Thought next to both. When the next one arrives mid-dog-walk, I open
the phone, type something fence-shaped into the finder, and the Page
answers by meaning, not by spelling — "marten", "weasel", "the thing
that keeps eating my fence" all land in the same place, because a
small model on my own machine understands they are the same worry.

So the garage door finally has what no scrolling app ever gave it: an
address. Not a smear of five hundred cousins who never met — one Page
that wears the whole history, every event filed against it, weighted
by the years instead of lost to them. Is the matching perfect? No,
and I will not pretend otherwise: it is aimed at perfect, and every
stupid thing I throw at it on this topic helps me aim better.

## So — am I alone?

The genius I will keep to myself, as promised. But if any of this
sounded less like my confession and more like your camera roll of
screenshots, your seventeen half-apps, your own five hundred garage
doors — then I was never alone in the stupidity, and that is the best
news this desk has had. Keep the folder, gain the address. The fence
is still standing, by the way. This week.
