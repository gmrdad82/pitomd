There are two ways to use an AI, and they look identical from across
the room. In both, a person types and a machine answers. The
difference is in who is holding the ideas. In the first way, you bring
a thought you actually have, and the machine accelerates it — finds
the counterexample, fetches the numbers, stress-tests the plan you
already meant to make. In the second way, you bring an empty prompt
and the machine fills it, and you nod along to fluent paragraphs you
would never have written, until one day you look at a month of your
own output and cannot find yourself in it.

I use AI daily, heavily, happily. And I hold one line like a
handrail: the machine works for my ideas. It does not get to have
them for me.

## Using AI for ideas starts with having some

The practice is unglamorous, which is how you can tell it works: I
think first, in writing, in my own plain files. The shed plan, the
stubborn opinion about studded tires, the half-theory about why the
game I am designing feels flat — they go into notes the ordinary way,
before any machine is consulted. This is not discipline for its own
sake. A thought you have written down is a thought you actually had;
it has edges, it can be disagreed with, it can be _worked on_. An
unwritten thought handed to an AI is not collaboration. It is
dictation, in the wrong direction.

Then, and only then, the machine gets invited — and what I hand it is
not a vague prompt but the thinking itself. In Said and Done., every
task points at the exact paragraphs of my notes it depends on, so
when an assistant joins a task, it reads precisely what I believe
and why: my numbers, my constraints, my opinions with their elbows
out. The answer that comes back is fitted to my actual thinking,
not to the average of the internet — because the question carried my
thinking with it. Generic in, generic out was always the whole
problem. The pins fix the "in".

The result reads differently, and you can feel it. Ask a bare AI how
to fix a fence and you get the world's most polite encyclopedia. Ask
it with your own three paragraphs pinned — the measurements, the
soil, the previous repair that failed — and you get an answer with
your fingerprints already on it. The ideas stayed mine. The legwork
stopped being.

## The door that reads but cannot write

Here is where the desk stops being scenery in this story and becomes
a load-bearing wall. When I built the door that lets AI agents into
Said and Done., I made it asymmetric on purpose: an agent can read my
notes — the pinned lines, the files, the context — but it cannot
write into them. Not a word. My notes folder is physically not
reachable through that door for writing, and this is not a settings
toggle I trust myself to keep flipped. It is how the door is built.

An agent has real hands elsewhere, and welcome to them: it can file
Pages, move them along, attach evidence, and leave Updates signed
with its own name, right next to my Thoughts. The working layer — the
tasks, the progress, the conversation — is genuinely shared. The
thinking layer is not. My files hold only sentences a human meant,
and every machine contribution lives above them, labeled, signed,
and separate, like annotations clipped to a manuscript rather than
ink on it.

That separation sounds philosophical until the day it is practical.
Months from now, when I reread a note, I never have to squint at a
paragraph wondering whether I believed that or whether something
fluent believed it near me. The provenance is structural. Everything
in the files is me. Everything from the machines says so, by name.
Using AI heavily without that line is how a notes folder slowly
turns into a transcript of someone else — the writing keeps
happening, but you stop being the one written.

## Keeping your own voice under acceleration

None of this is caution about the machines. It is confidence about
the human. The whole point of using AI for ideas is that your ideas
are the scarce ingredient — the machine can fetch, check, expand,
and draft against them at a speed no person can match, and that
trade only pays while the ideas keep being yours. Protect the
source, and the acceleration is pure gain. Let the source silt up
with generated sediment, and you are accelerating toward the
average.

So my practice, complete, in one paragraph: think in your own files
first. Hand the machine your actual thinking, pinned to the task,
not a prompt-shaped shrug. Let it work where work is shared — the
Pages, the legwork, the evidence — under its own signature. And
keep one layer of your life where no machine holds the pen, ever,
by construction rather than by willpower.

The dividend shows up somewhere unexpected: the writing itself.
Because my notes stayed mine, they still sound like me — the
half-jokes, the grudges against particular hardware stores, the
sentences my dogs would recognize if they could read. When I hand
that voice to an AI as context, what accelerates is _me_, recognizably,
just faster and better-researched. A year of heavy AI use, and I am
more in my notes, not less.

That is the test, in the end. Not how much you used the machine —
use it constantly, I do — but whether, reading back what you both
made, you can still find yourself without checking the signatures.
Keep the pen. Everything else is negotiable.
