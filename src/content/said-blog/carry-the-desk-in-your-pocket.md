Standing in aisle seven of the hardware store, holding two boxes of
screws, I needed one number: the measurement I wrote down last Tuesday at
my desk. This exact moment is why every notes app eventually grows a
cloud. Your thoughts get uploaded to someone's servers so that a phone
can reach them, and from then on your desk has a landlord: an account, a
subscription, a privacy policy that changes while you are asleep.

I wanted the aisle-seven moment without the landlord. So Said and Done.
carries its own phone door: the desktop app serves a full phone
client, from your computer, over a connection you own. My notes stayed
home. The desk still reached my pocket. The screws, for the record, were
the wrong ones anyway — but I knew that _in the store_, which was the
whole point.

## Serve your desk from your own machine

There is no companion service and nothing new to install. The desktop app
itself is the server: while it runs, it offers the phone client to
devices you have let in, and it speaks only to your own machine unless
you deliberately build a bridge outward: a tunnel or proxy of your
choosing, which is exactly how I reach my desk from anywhere. Building
that bridge is an evening project the first time and a habit forever
after; the app deliberately leaves it in your hands, because the moment
it picked a bridge for you, someone else would be holding one end. The
practical meaning of self-hosted here is blunt: nothing of yours is ever
parked on servers that are not yours, because there are no other servers
in the story at all.

Getting in is a small ceremony with real locks. In Settings you set a
password, add an optional one-time code — scan a QR with your
authenticator, the same ritual as your bank — and the app writes you an
access token it will show exactly once. It takes two minutes, happens
entirely inside the app's own window, and at no point does an email
address enter the conversation. You are not creating an
account with me — you are setting the locks on your own door, and the
difference shows in every detail: the token appears once and never again, the password is
yours to lose, and the only party who can reset any of it is the person
holding the computer. Your phone signs in and stays signed in for a
month at a time.

And now the part that sounds like a bug until it clicks: close the
desktop app, and the phone client goes dark. There is no background
service holding your desk open — window closed means desk closed,
everywhere at once. I have come to love this. My notes are reachable
when my computer is awake and serving them, and provably unreachable when
it is not.

## A real client, built for thumbs

This is not a desktop screen squeezed onto glass.
The phone client is its own thing, shaped around how a phone is actually
held and what a person actually does standing up.

You swipe your way between the four steps a Page walks, one at a time,
the motion your thumb already knows from everything else it does. Pages
open into the full dossier: description, the Thoughts and Updates, the
attached photos, the history of who did what. When a list grows long,
Pages group themselves under their first letters with a rail to jump by —
finding "fence" means tapping F, not scrolling through your whole
ambitious life. Filtering happens as you type, and the same little box
quick-adds a new Page when what you typed does not exist yet, which is
capture at the speed of a crosswalk.

The client sweats phone-specific details because half-hearted mobile
apps are a personal grievance of mine. Need to move six Pages along? A
multi-select mode does them as one batch instead of six pilgrimages. Deep
in a Page with the dog pulling the leash? The back button — the real
one, the one your thumb pressed on reflex — steps you out the way you
came instead of dumping you at the front door. Little toasts confirm
what just happened; the desk's single glass look follows you here
unchanged. None of
this is glamorous; it is simply the part most mobile companions skip,
and the part your thumb notices most.

My favorite hour with the phone client is not the store run. It is the
couch, later, when the day has gone soft. I swipe through what piled up:
the half-thoughts from the car, whatever four-word mysteries
afternoon-me left behind. Some get a line of description while
the context still breathes; some march forward a step; the mystery ones
get a Thought asking past-me what he meant, which he sometimes even
answers. Twenty minutes of thumb-work and tomorrow's desk is already
set. Triage never deserved a desk chair, and now it does not require
one.

## The desk leaves the house without leaving home

My phone now carries the whole desk and none of the compromise: every
Page, Thought, and pinned paragraph reachable from a store aisle or the far
end of the dog's favorite field, served by the computer at home with
nothing renting space in between. When an idea lands mid-walk, it goes
onto the record in the time a red light lasts, and it is simply there,
on the real desk rather than in an inbox awaiting import ceremony, when
I sit back down.
