My computer can simulate weather. It can render a city at a hundred and
forty frames a second, model the airflow over a wing, and decode more
video at once than any household should legally need. And for years,
the same machine visibly labored to open a note. A short text file,
the kind of document computers mastered before I was born, and there
was a spinner. Sometimes two spinners. Sometimes the note app needed a
moment "to sync" before it would let me read my own grocery thoughts.

Somewhere along the way we agreed to pretend this is normal. It is not
normal. It is a choice made by software, and different software can
choose differently.

## What a fast note taking app is actually made of

Speed is not a feature you sprinkle on at the end. It is the shape of
the thing, decided at the foundation, and Said and Done. was shaped
around one rule: the desk must never feel like it is somewhere else.

So there is no somewhere else. The app is one program running on your
machine, drawing its own pixels on your own graphics card — not a
browser wearing a costume, not a rented webpage phoning a rented
server. Your Pages live in one small database file in your home
folder. Your notes are your files on your disk. When you press a key,
the distance that keystroke travels is measured in silicon, not in
continents, and you can feel the difference before you can name it:
the letter rail answers as fast as your finger moves, a Page opens
like paper and not like a request, the switcher between Notebooks
keeps up with you typing at full temper.

The speed goes deeper than the drawing. The desk is frugal on
principle: when it indexes your notes, it tracks what actually changed
and never re-reads a file that has not moved — a decade-old folder is
indexed once, then followed, not chewed through nightly. Search across
everything answers while the second word is still leaving your hands.
Even the meaning search — the expensive kind, the kind other products
send to a data center — runs right there on your GPU, with a small
model that ships inside the installer. Which is the honest place to
say it plainly: the desk requires a real graphics card. No GPU, no
desk; a machine without one is told so at the door. I would rather
demand the hardware than fake the speed.

## Fast is a feeling about trust

I used to think I wanted speed because I am impatient. Then I watched
what slowness was actually doing to me, and it was worse than wasted
seconds: it was taxing the thoughts themselves.

A tool that hesitates teaches you to hesitate. When capturing an idea
costs a spinner, a sync, and a splash screen, some part of you starts
pre-filtering — is this thought worth the ceremony? Most thoughts
lose that audition. The half-formed ones, the silly ones, the ones
that turn out to matter in a month, they simply do not get written,
because the tool made writing feel like paperwork. Slowness is not a
performance problem. It is a censorship problem.

A desk that answers instantly runs the tax in reverse. Capture
becomes cheaper than forgetting, so I capture everything, and the
quality filter moves to where it belongs — later, at review, with the
thought safely on the record instead of judged at the door. The
number of ideas I write down went up the week I moved in, not because
I had more ideas but because fewer of them were being charged
admission.

Instant also means _trustworthy_ in a way I did not expect. When
every action lands the same millisecond you take it, the app stops
being a place where things might be happening and becomes a place
where things have plainly happened. Move a Page, it is moved. Type a
Thought, it is there. There is no "syncing…" limbo where your work
exists in some quantum in-between. The desk is never lying to you
about its own state, and after enough years of apps that were, that
honesty registers physically — shoulders, mostly.

## The machine you already paid for

The part that quietly offends me as a builder: none of this
required exotic engineering. It required respect. Your computer —
yes, even the mid-range laptop, even the desktop from a few years
back with a decent graphics card in it — is a marvel that spends most
of its life idling under software that treats it like a dumb terminal
from 1978. The machine was never the bottleneck. The disrespect was.

So the desk is built the way software used to be built when the
machine was expensive and the programmer was embarrassed to waste it:
one process, native pixels, local data, work done at home. The result
does not feel "optimized". It feels _calm_. Fast software, it turns
out, is quiet software — nothing spins, nothing skeleton-loads,
nothing apologizes. The desk simply keeps pace with your hands, and
your hands stop noticing the desk, which was the goal all along.

I still hear the objection: it is a notes app, who cares about
milliseconds? You do. You care hundreds of times a day, in a place
too deep to invoice. Every hesitation between you and a thought is a
small vote for not bothering, and a thinking tool has no business
casting that vote. Your computer has been ready to feel fast again
for years.

It was never the hardware. Ask more of your software — starting,
maybe, with the desk your notes deserve.
