I talk to AI assistants the way some people talk to contractors: often,
about specific jobs, and with a folder of context under my arm. For a
long time the routine was the same copy-paste errand: dig up the notes,
paste them into a chat, describe the task, carry the answer back by
hand, and do it again tomorrow with a chat that remembers none of it. The
assistant was smart. The doorway was dumb.

So I gave Said and Done. a proper door, and now my AI sits at the desk
with me. That is literal. A Claude session connects to my board, reads the
exact paragraphs my tasks are pinned to, adds and moves pages, and leaves
comments signed with its own name, while I do something better with my
hands. This post is about what an AI agents task board actually feels
like when it lives on your machine.

## Open the door once, in plain sight

The door is part of the app itself. Flip one toggle in Settings and Said
and Done. starts listening for AI tools on your own computer, and only
your own computer; nothing is exposed to the outside world. Next to the
toggle is a copy button holding the exact one-line command that
introduces the board to Claude. Paste it once in a terminal and the
introduction is made. The app's built-in Doctor screen probes that door
live and tells you it is open — down to how many tools are waiting
behind it — because a feature you cannot verify is a rumor.

What is on offer is the whole desk. A connected assistant can list your
chapters, search — with the same meaning-aware search you use — create
pages, walk them through their steps, comment, attach files, and read
history. The verbs it has are the verbs you have.

## Send the agent to the paragraph, not the pile

The tool I am proudest of is the humble one: an agent can ask for a
page's references and receive the _pinned lines themselves_ — the actual
text, each line numbered, with the file's full location attached. The
old way, an assistant either got whatever I
remembered to paste or went spelunking through my folder reading
everything, which is slow at best and none of its business at worst.

The pins change the errand completely. When I pinned three paragraphs to
my "choose winter tires" page months ago, I was leaving a trail for
future me; it turned out I was also leaving one for the assistant. Asked
to research that task, it went straight to those three paragraphs — my
mileage, my notes on our ice-rink of a street, my stubborn opinions
about studded tires — and came back with an answer that fit my car and
my winters, not a generic one. The agent reads your notes through the pins; it does not rewrite
them. Your files stay yours; the assistant gets a reading desk, never a
pen.

## Signed work, on a board you both share

Every agent at the desk works under a name. When a Claude session
connects, it introduces itself, and that identity follows everything it
does: every comment it leaves, every page it moves, every line in the
history. Open a page a day later and the thread reads like a small team's
correspondence: my question, the agent's findings underneath, a follow-up
from a different session under a different name. When something on the
board looks moved, the history says exactly who moved it, human or
otherwise. Trust in an assistant, it turns out, is mostly signatures.

The signatures also solve the amnesia. A fresh session, connecting for
the first time tomorrow, does not need me to re-explain the project:
it reads the page, the pinned paragraphs, the threads its predecessors
signed, and picks up where they stopped. The board remembers so that
neither of us has to, which is the same service it was already
performing for me.

Agents can bring receipts, too. A connected assistant can attach files
to a page the same way you do, so when one of mine finishes a job, the
evidence lands right on the card: the screenshot of the thing working,
the capture of the before and after. The page stops being a request I
wrote and becomes a small case file that closed itself: claim on top,
proof underneath, and a name against both.

## A chair at the desk, not a window to shout through

The rhythm this settles into surprised me. The board became the meeting
place. I write pages the ordinary way, pin the ordinary paragraphs, and
then the assistant and I work the same pages — sometimes at the same
time, me editing a description while it drops research into a comment two
cards over. Nothing about the board had to become special to allow this.
A page was always a little dossier of intent plus evidence; it reads as
well to a language model as it does to a person.

Because the whole arrangement lives on my machine, the questions that
usually shadow letting an AI near your notes just do not arise. A
session ends and the door is still mine.

Yesterday's version of me pasted paragraphs into chat windows like a
clerk feeding a fax machine. Today's version writes a task, pins the
thinking, and says: work through these, leave notes. Later, after the
actual coffee and the actual errands, the board holds the results, signed and
sitting exactly where the work belongs, next to the paragraphs that
prompted it.

The contractor finally gets the folder — and returns it with the invoice
annotated.
